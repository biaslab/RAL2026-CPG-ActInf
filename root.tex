\begin{abstract}
Central pattern generators (CPGs) produce efficient rhythmic locomotion from a small set of parameters, but a set tuned for one terrain is no longer optimal once the terrain changes.
We show that the CPG parameters yielding stable walking on flat ground and on a $10^{\circ}$ incline are distinct, and that on a corridor of repeated transitions keeping the flat-optimal set more than doubles the fall rate relative to the terrain-matched optimum, so that resuming stable locomotion requires online adaptation.
We propose an event-triggered scheme in which a prediction-error monitor detects each terrain change and re-tunes the CPG within the same walking bout, and we compare grid search, Bayesian optimization, and an autoregressive active inference agent that minimizes expected free energy.
Grid search and Bayesian optimization destabilize the robot while probing candidate gaits, roughly doubling the fall rate, whereas the active inference agent selects parameters from a learned model without such trials and avoids this harm.
Aligning its goal prior with the stability criterion, rather than with velocity tracking, makes it significantly outperform keeping the incumbent gait on a sustained transition, with the information-seeking term of the free energy essential to this gain; generalizing the improvement across repeated transitions remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

% Efficient locomotion is crucial for animal survival, allowing predator evasion, foraging, and mating. Over millions of years, natural selection has refined both the morphologies and neural control systems of animals, allowing them to navigate in complex and unpredictable conditions with notable efficiency \cite{dickinson2000how}.

Deploying legged machines in constantly changing environments demands an adaptability that engineers have long sought by replicating animal morphologies, such as snake-like robots for constrained spaces or quadruped platforms for irregular terrain \cite{Pettersen2017SnakeRobots, dwivedula2025configbot}. These platforms inherit the mechanical advantages of their biological counterparts, but their control systems often do not: the Boston Dynamics quadruped Spot, for instance, relies on centralized, sensor-intensive controllers that are effective yet complex and expensive, limiting wider deployment \cite{dwivedula2025configbot}.

Central pattern generators (CPGs) offer a simpler alternative. In vertebrates, these spinal neural circuits generate the periodic, coordinated signals of movements such as walking, freeing higher brain centers to modulate speed and gait through simple inputs while sensory feedback keeps the rhythm coupled to the body and environment \cite{goulding2009circuits, dickinson2000how}. This decentralized architecture reduces command latency and bandwidth and simplifies high-level control \cite{CPG-Review}, making CPG-based controllers less energy intensive, simpler, and more affordable.


\subsection{Related Work}

Among CPG models used in robotics, coupled nonlinear oscillators are biologically plausible and computationally efficient \cite{Richmond1994, CPG-Review}. Their mutual inhibition synchronizes the joints into coordinated gaits with smooth transitions between them \cite{Richmond1994, PassiveCompliant2008, righetti2008pattern}, and their limit-cycle behavior yields self-sustaining oscillations that recover quickly from perturbations \cite{PassiveCompliant2008}. Governed by only a few parameters such as frequency and amplitude, they suit embedded systems with limited resources, yet most implementations still target high-end platforms, leaving their use on compact systems underexplored.

A complementary line of work tunes gait parameters as black-box optimization, from measured performance rather than a model of the dynamics. Bayesian optimization suits this setting, where each walking trial is expensive: it fits a Gaussian-process surrogate to the parameter-score pairs seen so far and proposes the next parameters by maximizing an acquisition function such as the upper confidence bound. Run \emph{online}, it scores the parameters of the most recent window, augments the surrogate, and proposes the next window's parameters, refining the gait during walking; a trust region around the current best keeps proposals from jumping to destabilizing gaits \cite{eriksson2019turbo}. We adopt this online Bayesian optimization as an adaptation baseline in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

In the following, we contribute
\begin{itemize}
    \item evidence that terrain-matched CPG parameters confer a durable survival advantage across repeated terrain transitions, so that resuming stable locomotion calls for online adaptation;
    \item an event-triggered adaptation scheme, driven by a re-arming prediction-error monitor, together with the finding that online black-box gait optimization (grid search and Bayesian optimization) is actively destructive on hazardous transitions; and
    \item an autoregressive active inference agent that avoids this destabilization by selecting from a learned model, and the result that aligning its goal prior with the stability criterion is what turns this caution into a significant improvement over the incumbent gait on a sustained transition, with robust adaptation across recurring transitions identified as an open problem.
\end{itemize}

\section{PROBLEM STATEMENT}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

We first describe the base platform whose motion defines the locomotion objective, and then the central pattern generator that produces the coordinated leg motion driving it.
We then argue that the parameters yielding stable locomotion depend on the terrain, so that a parameter set tuned for one terrain degrades once the terrain changes.
This motivates online adaptation of the parameters as a requirement for resuming stable locomotion.

\subsection{Base platform and locomotion objective}
\label{sec:base-platform}

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering

\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{noadapt_instability}
\caption{Keeping the flat-optimal parameters after a terrain change destabilizes the robot. \emph{Left}: the trunk tilt from vertical stays small on flat ground; after the transition (dotted line) both sets settle near the incline baseline, but keeping the flat optimum lets the tilt run away past the fall threshold (dashed line, $\times$), whereas switching to the incline optimum holds it near the baseline. \emph{Right}: the robot climbs steadily after switching, whereas keeping the flat-optimal parameters ends in a fall and halted progress. Curves are one representative seed.}
\label{fig:noadapt}

The robot is a floating base actuated through its legs.
We describe the trunk by its base state
\begin{align}
  \xi = (p, \Psi, v, \omega) \,,
  \label{eq:base-state}
\end{align}
collecting the linear position $p \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and the angular position (orientation) $\Psi \in SO(3)$ of the trunk in a world frame, together with the linear velocity $v = \dot{p}$ and the angular velocity $\omega$.
The orientation $\Psi$ is parameterized by the roll, pitch, and yaw $(\psi_r, \psi_p, \psi_y)$, of which the roll and pitch measure the deviation from an upright attitude.

The legs couple this base state to the joints.
For a given motion of the base there is a corresponding configuration of the twelve joints, their rates, and the torques required to realize it,
\begin{align}
  (\varphi, \dot{\varphi}, \tau) = f(\xi) \,,
  \label{eq:base-to-joints}
\end{align}
where $\varphi$ are the joint angles, $\dot{\varphi}$ their rates, and $\tau$ the joint torques.
The map $f$ is unknown: it depends on the full rigid-body dynamics, the terrain, and the switching contact between the feet and the ground, none of which are available in closed form.

Quadrupedal locomotion is then the problem of finding the joint torques $\tau$ such that the base state follows a desired behavior.
Concretely, we require the trunk to maintain a target forward velocity $v^\star$ while holding an upright angular position, that is roll and pitch near zero,
\begin{align}
  \tau^\star = \arg\min_{\tau} \; \big\lVert v - v^\star \big\rVert + \big\lVert (\psi_r, \psi_p) \big\rVert \,.
  \label{eq:locomotion-objective}
\end{align}
Because $f$ is unknown, these torques cannot be solved for from a model directly.
Instead, a central pattern generator produces rhythmic joint-angle references that a joint-level controller tracks through torques, shaping the base motion toward the objective, which we introduce next.

\subsection{Central Pattern Generator}

The CPGs are modeled as a network of four mutually coupled Hopf oscillators, each driving one limb: left front (LF), right front (RF), left hind (LH), and right hind (RH). Coordination between limbs arises from inhibitory coupling through a gait-specific coupling matrix $K = [k_{ij}]$.
Let $\omega_i$ be the balance between stance and swing frequencies, operationalized as
\begin{equation}
	\omega_i = \omega_{\mathrm{stance}} \varsigma(-b y_i)  + \omega_{\mathrm{swing}} \varsigma(b y_i) \, , \label{eq:omega_i}
\end{equation}
with $\varsigma(\cdot)$ a sigmoid function. Then, the oscillators evolve as
\begin{align}
  \dot{x}_i &= \alpha\,(u - r_i^2)\,x_i - \omega_i\,y_i, \\
  \dot{y}_i &= \beta\,(u - r_i^2)\,y_i + \omega_i\,x_i
               + \gamma \sum_{j=1}^4 k_{ij}\,y_j + s_i, \label{eq:ydot} \, ,
\end{align}
where $r_i = \sqrt{x_i^2 + y_i^2}$, $u = 2$ is the fixed squared limit-cycle amplitude (a constant, not tuned), $\alpha, \beta > 0$ are convergence rates, $\gamma$ is the coupling gain, and $b$ controls the sharpness of the frequency transition between stance and swing phases.

The final term $s_i$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:ydot} is the contact-driven sensory feedback, and it is where the fast-contact gain $F_{\text{fast}}$ and the stop gain $K_{\text{stop}}$ enter the dynamics.
It couples each oscillator to its leg's stance or swing phase, read from $y_i$ by hysteresis, and to the binary foot-contact signal $c_i \in \{0, 1\}$:
\begin{align}
    s_i = \begin{cases}
        K_{\text{stop}}\Big(\omega_i x_i - \gamma \sum_{j} k_{ij} y_j\Big),
            & \text{stop},\\[2pt]
        F_{\text{fast}}\,\operatorname{sgn}(y_i), & \text{fast},\\[2pt]
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:feedback}
\end{align}
The \emph{stop} case applies when phase and contact disagree, that is a swing phase with no contact ($c_i = 0$) or a stance phase with contact ($c_i = 1$), and $K_{\text{stop}}$ scales a correction that arrests the oscillator; the \emph{fast} case applies otherwise during swing or stance, where $F_{\text{fast}}$ advances it in the direction of $y_i$; the feedback is zero during the transition between phases.
This is a Righetti-style load feedback that keeps the rhythm entrained to ground contact \cite{righetti2008pattern}.

The CPG outputs $(x_i, y_i)$ are mapped to hip and knee joint angles:
\begin{align}
\varphi_{\text{hip}} &= \varphi_{\text{hip}}^{0} + A_{\text{hip}}\, x_i , \\
\varphi_{\text{knee}} &= \varphi_{\text{knee}}^{0} - A_{\text{knee}}\, \max(0, y_i) ,
\end{align}
where $x_i$ drives the forward-backward hip swing and $y_i$ controls knee flexion, the fixed offsets $\varphi_{\text{hip}}^{0}$ and $\varphi_{\text{knee}}^{0}$ set the neutral pose, the gains $A_{\text{hip}}$ and $A_{\text{knee}}$ scale the swing and flexion amplitudes, and negative $y_i$ is suppressed to prevent hyperextension.

The behavior of the CPG is governed by a small set of parameters, collected in the vector
\begin{align}
    \theta = [\gamma, \omega_{\text{swing}}, \omega_{\text{stance}}, F_{\text{fast}}, K_{\text{stop}}, A_{\text{hip}}, A_{\text{knee}}, b] \,,
    \label{eq:cpg-parameters}
\end{align}
comprising the coupling gain, the phase-dependent stance and swing frequencies, the fast-contact gain $F_{\text{fast}}$ and stop gain $K_{\text{stop}}$ of the sensory feedback (Eq.~\ref{eq:feedback}), the hip and knee amplitudes, and the sigmoid sharpness $b$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:omega_i}.
These parameters set the gait shape, and are held fixed during locomotion.

\subsection{Terrain-dependent parameters}
\label{sec:terrain-dependent}

For a fixed terrain, the parameter vector $\theta$ can be tuned to produce stable locomotion.
Let $e$ denote the terrain.
Running the CPG with parameters $\theta$ on terrain $e$ induces a distribution $p(\xi \given \theta, e)$ over the base state, which we score by the locomotion objective of Eq.~\ref{eq:locomotion-objective},
\begin{align}
    V(\theta, e) = - \mathbb{E}_{p(\xi \given \theta, e)} \Big[ \frac{1}{v^\star} \big\lVert v - v^\star \big\rVert + \frac{1}{\psi_0} \big\lVert (\psi_r, \psi_p) \big\rVert \Big] \,,
    \label{eq:criterion}
\end{align}
setting $V = -c_f$ whenever the robot falls.
The scale $\psi_0$ lets the attitude term dominate, encoding that remaining upright is the primary requirement.
In practice the expectation is a time average over an evaluation window, computed on low-pass-filtered angles so that the gait's rhythmic rocking is not penalized, with the pitch measured relative to the terrain incline and progress beyond the target speed not rewarded.
The best gait for terrain $e$ is then the terrain-conditioned optimum
\begin{align}
    \theta^{\star}(e) = \underset{\theta}{\arg\max}\; V(\theta, e) \,.
    \label{eq:terrain-optimum}
\end{align}

The difficulty is that $\theta^{\star}(e)$ is not constant across terrains.
We consider two terrains, flat ground and a $10^{\circ}$ incline, and maximize Eq.~\ref{eq:criterion} on each with Bayesian optimization.
The resulting optima differ markedly: relative to the flat optimum, the incline optimum pushes the coupling gain $\gamma$, both frequencies, the fast-contact gain $F_{\rm fast}$, and the transition sharpness $b$ to the top of their ranges while roughly halving the stop gain $K_{\rm stop}$; only the joint amplitudes are unchanged.
The incline gait couples the limbs more strongly, swings the legs faster, and switches more sharply between stance and swing, and each parameter set is more stable on the terrain it was tuned for.
The cost of the mismatch grows with exposure: on a single transition it is transient, roughly doubling the early fall rate before survivors settle, but on a corridor of repeated $10^{\circ}$ transitions keeping the flat optimum leads to falls in $26\%$ of runs against $11\%$ with the incline optimum, a gap that holds over the whole traverse (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

Because $\theta$ is held fixed, a change in terrain leaves the controller at a now-suboptimal point $\theta^{\star}(e_{\text{old}}) \neq \theta^{\star}(e_{\text{new}})$, and the resulting attitude deviations compound: as the trunk tilts away from balance, recovery demands larger corrections, and locomotion can fail before the new terrain is traversed.
Fig.~\ref{fig:noadapt} shows a representative bout: after the transition, holding the flat-optimal parameters lets the tilt run away past the fall threshold and the robot topples, whereas switching to the incline-optimal parameters keeps the tilt near the incline baseline and the robot climbs steadily.
Resuming stable locomotion after a terrain change therefore requires detecting that the terrain has changed and re-tuning $\theta$ toward $\theta^{\star}(e_{\text{new}})$ online, quickly enough to preserve balance.


\section{METHODS}
\label{sec:methods}


\subsection{Autoregressive active inference agent}
\label{sec:agent}

The agent models the robot as a multivariate autoregressive process with exogenous inputs (MARX) \cite{nisslbeck2024coupled}.
The observation $y_k = (v_x, v_y, \psi_p, \psi_r)$ is a linear-Gaussian function of a regressor $x_k = [\,u_k,\ \bar{u}_k,\ \bar{y}_k\,]^{\intercal}$ that stacks the current control $u_k = \theta$ with short memories $\bar{u}_k, \bar{y}_k$ of past inputs and outputs,
\begin{align} \label{eq:likelihood}
    p(y_k \given \Theta, x_k) = \cN\big(y_k \given A^{\intercal} x_k,\, W^{-1} \big) ,
\end{align}
with parameters $\Theta = (A, W)$.
A matrix-normal-Wishart prior is conjugate to this likelihood \cite[ID: P323]{Soch}, so the belief over $\Theta$ is updated in closed form by rank-one Bayesian filtering as each pair $(x_k, y_k)$ arrives, and marginalizing it gives a Student-t posterior predictive whose mean and covariance,
\begin{align} \label{eq:postpred}
    \mu_k(u) = M_{k-1}^{\intercal} x_k , \qquad
    \Sigma_k(u) = \frac{1 + x_k^{\intercal} \Lambda_{k-1}^{-1} x_k}{\nu_{k-1} - D_y - 1}\, \Omega_{k-1} ,
\end{align}
are smooth functions of the control $u$ through the regressor $x_k$.
Here $M, \Lambda, \Omega, \nu$ are the filtered posterior parameters and $D_y = \dim y$; we take the recursions as standard for this conjugate model \cite{nisslbeck2024coupled, Soch} and keep the focus on the control objective.

The agent selects controls by minimizing an expected free energy.
Two priors close the model: a control prior $p(u) = \cN(u \given \mu_0, \Upsilon^{-1})$ regularizes the parameters toward a reference gait $\mu_0$ with precision $\Upsilon$, and a goal prior $\bar{p}(y) = \cN(y \given m_{*}, S_{*})$ encodes the target observation, the desired forward velocity $v^{\star}$ at an upright attitude, with $S_{*}$ setting how tightly each channel is enforced.
Planning over a horizon $\tau = \{k{+}1, \dots, k{+}T\}$ unrolls the model, substituting predictive means for the not-yet-observed outputs, and minimizes the cumulative divergence of the predicted observations from the goal prior,
\begin{align} \label{eq:EFE}
    G(u_\tau) = \sum_{t \in \tau} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big[\, p(y_t \given u_t, \mathcal{D}_k) \,\big\|\, \bar{p}(y_t) \,\big] .
\end{align}
Expanding the divergence and adding the control prior gives the objective minimized at each planning step,
\begin{align} \label{eq:total-MAP}
    \hat{u}_\tau = \underset{u_\tau}{\arg\min} \sum_{t \in \tau} \Big[\,
        &\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\tr\!\big(S_{*}^{-1} \Sigma_t(u_t)\big) - \tfrac{1}{2}\ln| \Sigma_t(u_t) |}_{\text{information gain}} \nonumber \\[-2pt]
        + \; &\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mu_t(u_t) - m_{*}\big)^{\intercal} S_{*}^{-1} \big(\mu_t(u_t) - m_{*}\big)}_{\text{goal seeking}} \nonumber \\[-2pt]
        + \; &\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\big(u_t - \mu_0\big)^{\intercal} \Upsilon \big(u_t - \mu_0\big)}_{\text{control cost}} \,\Big] .
\end{align}
The information-gain terms, through the predictive covariance $\Sigma_t$, drive the agent to resolve model uncertainty; the goal-seeking term, a Mahalanobis distance of the predicted mean $\mu_t$ from the target $m_{*}$, drives it toward stable walking; and the control cost keeps it near the reference gait.

\subsection{Prediction-error triggering}
\label{sec:trigger}

Re-tuning $\theta$ at every step is wasteful and, as Section~\ref{sec:experiments} shows, actively destabilizing, so the agent adapts only when the incumbent gait is no longer matched to the terrain.
We detect that event by testing, online, whether the incumbent controls still minimize the control objective of Eq.~\ref{eq:total-MAP}.

Consider the one-step objective $G(u)$ under two simplifications that hold in the monitoring regime, once the gait is steady and the belief identified.
The control cost is a fixed price rather than a signal, and the information-gain term is flat in $u$ (its only dependence is through $\ln(1 + x^{\intercal}\Lambda^{-1}x)$, negligible for large $\Lambda$).
What remains is the goal cross-entropy
\begin{align} \label{eq:trigger-ce}
    G(u) = \tfrac{1}{2}\tr\!\big(S_{*}^{-1}\Sigma\big) + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mu(u) - m_{*}\big)^{\intercal} S_{*}^{-1} \big(\mu(u) - m_{*}\big) ,
\end{align}
with $\mu(u) = M^{\intercal} x$ affine in $u$ through the learned control-to-output gain $B := \partial \mu / \partial u$, a block of $M^{\intercal}$.
The incumbent $\theta$ remains terrain-matched precisely when it is a stationary point of $G$.
Because $\mu$ is affine in $u$ and $G$ is quadratic in $\mu$, $G$ is exactly convex-quadratic in $u$, with gradient and Hessian
\begin{align} \label{eq:grad-hess}
    g = \cmnabla_u G(\theta) = B^{\intercal} S_{*}^{-1}(\mu - m_{*}) , \qquad
    H = \cmnabla_u^2 G = B^{\intercal} S_{*}^{-1} B .
\end{align}
The value $G(\theta)$ alone does not test optimality: the goal error $\mu - m_{*}$ can grow along directions the controls cannot affect, terrain error that $B$ annihilates, leaving $\theta$ stationary despite a high cross-entropy.
The gradient separates the fixable error from the rest.
Its affine-invariant magnitude is the Newton decrement
\begin{align} \label{eq:newton}
    \lambda^2 = g^{\intercal} H^{-1} g = \big\lVert \Pi_{\mathcal{R}(B)}(\mu - m_{*}) \big\rVert^2_{S_{*}^{-1}} ,
\end{align}
the squared goal error projected onto the controllable subspace $\mathcal{R}(B)$.
Since $G$ is exactly quadratic, $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^2 = G(\theta) - \min_u G(u)$ is not a local approximation but the exact suboptimality gap, the cross-entropy a full re-tuning of the gait would recover.
Triggering on $\lambda^2$ therefore fires on \emph{fixable} mismatch rather than on raw prediction error, and correctly stays silent when the error lies in the uncontrollable subspace, where no gait switch would help.

The threshold follows from the same statistic.
Under the null hypothesis that the incumbent is still optimal, the goal error is predictive noise, $\mu - m_{*} \sim \cN(0, \Sigma)$, so $\lambda^2$ is a quadratic form of a Gaussian and hence (generalized) $\chi^2$ with $r = \operatorname{rank}(B) \le \min(D_u, D_y)$ degrees of freedom.
A target false-alarm rate $\alpha$ then fixes
\begin{align} \label{eq:chi2}
    K = \chi^2_r(1 - \alpha) ,
\end{align}
a threshold with units and a calibrated meaning in place of a hand-tuned level.
Equivalently, restoring the control cost as the price of adapting gives a decision-theoretic rule: trigger only when the recoverable gain outweighs the cost of the corrective step $\Delta u = -H^{-1} g$, that is $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^2 > \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta u^{\intercal}\Upsilon\,\Delta u$, so that adaptation fires exactly when the full expected free energy would decrease.

In practice, calibrating the null scale of $\lambda^2$ on a short flat-walking prefix reduces the test to a dimensionless ratio near unity during nominal locomotion, the form used in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
The attitude channels of $S_{*}$ are tightened relative to the velocity channels so the statistic responds to a loss of balance rather than to the slower climb up the incline, and it is smoothed by an exponential moving average to reject stride-frequency ripple.
An event fires when the smoothed statistic exceeds $K$ for $P$ consecutive steps; the monitor then disarms and re-arms only once it falls back well below $K$, so a single terrain change produces one event while a corridor of repeated changes re-triggers once per transition.
Symmetrically, adaptation is paused whenever the statistic returns below $K$, so re-tuning runs only while the incumbent is provably suboptimal.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}
\label{sec:experiments}

We test whether \emph{event-triggered} online adaptation of the CPG parameters can restore stable locomotion after a terrain change, and we compare three parameter-selection strategies as the adaptation mechanism: a \emph{grid search} that samples the parameter space by Latin-hypercube design, online \emph{Bayesian optimization} (BO) with a Gaussian-process surrogate and a UCB acquisition function, and the proposed \emph{MARX-EFE} agent of Section~\ref{sec:methods}.
Grid search and BO are black-box optimizers that can only assess a candidate gait by walking on it, whereas MARX-EFE selects parameters from its continually updated model without such trials.
The comparison targets the transient after the terrain change: the hypothesis is that the method which finds better parameters faster keeps the robot upright, because a growing attitude deviation is progressively harder to correct.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

Experiments use the Laikago quadruped in PyBullet at a control rate of $100$\,Hz ($\Delta t = 0.01$\,s).
The robot walks along a corridor that is flat for the first stretch and then rises at a $10^{\circ}$ incline.
It starts with the flat-optimal parameters $\theta^{\star}(\text{flat})$ and, after $10$\,s of flat walking, meets the incline; the incline is placed at the robot's forward position at that instant, measured per seed by a calibration run, so that every run enters the slope in the same phase of its gait.
The reference optima $\theta^{\star}(\text{flat})$ and $\theta^{\star}(10^{\circ})$ are obtained beforehand by maximizing the criterion of Eq.~\ref{eq:criterion} on each terrain with Bayesian optimization, using $v^{*} = 0.5$\,m/s, $\psi_0 = 10^{\circ}$, and a fall penalty $c_f = 2$.

We run $N = 100$ seeds.
Across-seed variability comes only from a small perturbation of the initial joint angles ($\sigma = 0.002$\,rad), which the contact dynamics amplify into distinct trajectories; the start pose and the terrain geometry are otherwise identical across seeds, which isolates the response to the terrain change from the choice of starting point.

\subsection{Detecting the terrain change}
\label{sec:exp-trigger}

Adaptation is triggered by the optimality monitor of Section~\ref{sec:trigger}.
A MARX belief (Eq.~\ref{eq:likelihood}) is filtered online from the observation $y_k = (v_x, v_y, \psi_p, \psi_r)$ with forgetting factor $0.995$, under a stability-oriented goal prior whose attitude channels are tightened, a $20^{\circ}$ standard deviation on pitch to tolerate the steady incline offset and $6^{\circ}$ on roll, so the statistic responds to a loss of balance rather than to the slower climb.
At each step the monitor evaluates the Newton decrement $\lambda^2$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:newton}), the goal cross-entropy that re-tuning the gait could recover, from the model's steady-state control gain $B$.
Because $B$ is the multi-step feedthrough of the identified model, $\lambda^2$ reflects the sustained mismatch the incline creates, whereas a one-step innovation at $100$\,Hz is dominated by persistence and barely registers the change.
An event fires when $\lambda^2$ exceeds the decision-theoretic budget of Section~\ref{sec:trigger}, the control-prior price of a reference gait move of $2\%$ of each parameter's range, which for the monitor's control-prior width evaluates to $\tau \approx 2.3$; unlike a normalized error ratio, this threshold carries units and needs no per-seed flat-walking baseline.
On flat ground the incumbent is optimal and $\lambda^2$ stays below $\tau$; when the trunk crosses onto the incline the controllable part of the goal error grows and $\lambda^2$ crosses $\tau$ shortly after, firing once.
For the repeated-transition corridor of Section~\ref{sec:exp-repeated} the monitor re-arms once $\lambda^2$ falls back below $\tfrac{1}{2}\tau$, so it fires once per ramp rather than latching.

\subsection{Adaptation protocol}
\label{sec:exp-protocol}

When the trigger fires, the selected method begins proposing CPG parameters, one candidate per $1.5$\,s window, ramped in over $0.3$\,s, and each window is scored by Eq.~\ref{eq:criterion}.
The optimizers search a trust region around the incumbent and share a common safety layer: the best-scoring parameters seen so far are restored for one window whenever a candidate scores well below them.
Without the safeguard, every method, MARX-EFE included, destabilizes the robot within seconds, so it is applied uniformly across methods.
The trust-region radius, however, interacts with $\theta^{\star}$: at $\pm 0.25$ of the parameter range the incline optimum is not reachable, because $\theta^{\star}(10^{\circ})$ raises the coupling gain $\gamma$ across its full range and drops the sharpness $b$, so only $30\%$ of the flat-to-incline shift lies inside the box.
We therefore report both the tight $\pm 0.25$ region, which equalizes caution across the black-box optimizers, and a full-range region in which $\theta^{\star}(10^{\circ})$ is reachable, which is required to ask whether a method can adapt toward it.
MARX-EFE uses a forgetting factor of $0.995$ so that its model tracks the change, and its control prior is centered on the current parameters.
Its goal prior encodes the target observation; we compare the original prior, which rewards the target forward velocity with a loose attitude term, against a \emph{stability-aligned} prior whose attitude term is tightened to the $\psi_0 = 10^{\circ}$ scale of the criterion, so that the expected-free-energy objective targets the attitude that $\theta^{\star}$ optimizes.

All arms share an identical pre-trigger trajectory per seed so that the event fires at the same instant in every arm.
Two references bracket the online methods: \emph{no adaptation} keeps $\theta^{\star}(\text{flat})$, a lower reference, and the \emph{oracle} switches directly to $\theta^{\star}(10^{\circ})$, an upper reference that presumes the new optimum is known.
The online methods are \emph{grid}, \emph{BO}, and the proposed \emph{MARX-EFE}, the last with either the velocity or the stability-aligned goal prior; we write \emph{MARX-EFE-S} for the stability-aligned variant.
We report the fall rate over the $20$\,s after the trigger, the fall rate over the first $10$\,s (the transition phase, where the parameter mismatch itself is the hazard), the mean time the robot stays upright, and the mean windowed criterion $\bar{V}$.

Table~\ref{tab:eventtrigger} and Fig.~\ref{fig:eventtrigger} summarize the single-transition comparison at the $\pm 0.25$ trust region.
The shift in the optimum matters at the transition: within the first $10$\,s the oracle falls in $8\%$ of seeds against $19\%$ for no adaptation (McNemar $p = 0.03$), so switching to terrain-matched parameters roughly halves the early falls.

Trial-based search is destructive when run online.
Grid and BO must spend whole windows walking on speculative candidates, and on an incline a poor candidate does not merely score badly, it tips the robot over.
Even confined to the trust region and protected by the safeguard, this roughly doubles the fall rate relative to not adapting ($83\%$ and $90\%$ against $40\%$; McNemar $p \le 4\times10^{-10}$, upright-time Wilcoxon $p \approx 10^{-12}$).
MARX-EFE avoids these physical trials by selecting from its model, and stays far below the black-box optimizers (early $10\%$ against $37\%$, $p < 10^{-5}$).
Over the full horizon, however, it matches no adaptation ($39\%$ against $40\%$, not significant): with the velocity goal prior it avoids harm but does not improve on holding the incumbent.

The parameter trajectories explain why (Fig.~\ref{fig:theta}).
Under the velocity goal prior the selected $\theta(t)$ stays at $\theta^{\star}(\text{flat})$: MARX-EFE does not move toward the incline optimum.
Two factors are responsible.
The tight trust region does not contain $\theta^{\star}(10^{\circ})$, so the incline optimum is unreachable; but widening it does not help either, and the deeper cause is the goal prior, which rewarded forward velocity with only a loose ($45^{\circ}$) attitude term and so did not target the attitude stability that $\theta^{\star}$ optimizes.
Ablations confirm that the machinery was idle under this goal: removing the epistemic term (a greedy predictive selector), removing the forgetting factor, or removing the safeguard each leaves the outcome statistically unchanged, and re-centering the control prior away from the incumbent only destabilizes the robot.
The safeguard is thus load-bearing for grid and BO, which wander, but not for MARX-EFE, which holds the incumbent regardless.

Aligning the goal prior with the stability criterion changes this.
Table~\ref{tab:stable} reports the effect of tightening the attitude term to the $\psi_0 = 10^{\circ}$ scale (MARX-EFE-S), with the trust region widened so that $\theta^{\star}(10^{\circ})$ is reachable.
MARX-EFE-S falls in $27\%$ of runs over $20$\,s against $40\%$ for no adaptation (McNemar $p = 0.04$) and in $7\%$ against $19\%$ over the first $10$\,s ($p = 0.008$), the first arm to improve significantly on holding the incumbent, and below the oracle as well ($p = 0.006$).
It achieves this not by reaching $\theta^{\star}(10^{\circ})$, which stays out of range of its small adjustments, but by making minor attitude-directed corrections around the incumbent: a nearby, model-guided change suffices to keep the trunk upright.
The epistemic term is now load-bearing: with it removed the same goal prior yields $42\%$, no better than no adaptation.
The active-inference machinery therefore contributes, but only once its goal prior encodes the quantity being optimized.

\subsection{Repeated transitions}
\label{sec:exp-repeated}

A single incline is a mild test of adaptation, because a fixed gait that survives the transition can coast through the rest of the bout.
We therefore repeat the experiment on a corridor of two consecutive $10^{\circ}$ ramps separated by flat landings, so that the robot meets the terrain change more than once and a mismatched gait is punished repeatedly.
Here the value of terrain-matched parameters is both larger and durable: the oracle falls in $11\%$ of runs against $26\%$ for no adaptation, and the gap holds over the whole traverse rather than washing out as it did on the single incline.
The optimality monitor re-arms once $\lambda^2$ falls back below the budget after each ramp, detecting every transition without firing on the flat landings between them.
Online black-box search remains destructive: grid and BO fall in $87\%$ and $92\%$ of runs at the full-range trust region ($38\%$ and $41\%$ even when confined to the tight region), far above both references.

The model-based agent does not capture the oracle's advantage here, however (Table~\ref{tab:repeated}).
With either goal prior it falls in $23$--$24\%$ of runs, statistically indistinguishable from no adaptation ($26\%$, McNemar $p = 0.83$) and well above the oracle ($11\%$, $p = 0.007$).
The stability-aligned prior that improved on the single sustained incline does not replicate that gain when the transitions recur, and the epistemic term confers no advantage in this setting either.
A gait corrected for one ramp is not necessarily right for the next after an intervening flat stretch, and the small corrections that sufficed on a single incline do not reach the alternating optimum.
Capturing the oracle's durable benefit across repeated transitions, rather than only avoiding the destabilization that black-box search causes, therefore remains open.

\section{DISCUSSION}

Our results separate two aspects of online adaptation that are easily conflated: deciding \emph{when} to change the gait and deciding \emph{what} to change it to.
The optimality trigger resolves the first cheaply and reliably, firing on the controllable part of the prediction error, once per terrain change and never on flat ground, and it re-arms to detect a sequence of transitions.
The second is where the methods diverge.
Grid search and Bayesian optimization can only judge a candidate gait by walking on it, and on an incline a poor candidate does not merely score badly, it tips the robot over; even confined to a trust region and protected by a revert-to-best safeguard, this exploration roughly doubles the fall rate relative to not adapting at all.
The active inference agent instead selects parameters from a model that it updates every control step, so it avoids these destabilizing trials.
What the agent selects, however, depends critically on its goal prior.
With a goal that tracks forward velocity, the agent simply holds the incumbent gait: it avoids harm but does not improve on not adapting, and its information-seeking term, forgetting factor, and revert-to-best safeguard are all idle.
Once the goal prior encodes attitude stability, matching the criterion that defines the terrain optimum, the agent instead makes small model-guided corrections that significantly reduce falls on a sustained transition, and the information-seeking term becomes essential to that gain.
The advantage is therefore not faster search, but the ability to improve the gait without risking it, and it appears only when the agent's objective encodes the quantity being optimized.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions.
The improvement does not yet generalize: on a corridor of repeated transitions, where the terrain-matched optimum confers a large and durable advantage, neither the stability-aligned agent nor any goal-prior setting we swept captures it, and the selected parameters never travel to the terrain optimum.
The barrier appears structural rather than a matter of tuning: the linear autoregressive model supports small local corrections but cannot predict which distant parameter settings are stable, and loosening the control prior to permit larger moves only reintroduces destabilizing exploration.
Robust adaptation across recurring terrain changes, for instance by re-selecting per transition or by a model expressive enough to navigate to the terrain optimum, is the central open problem.
The study is also in simulation with reference optima computed offline, and the open-loop CPG has a narrow terrain envelope: beyond roughly $10^{\circ}$ of incline, and on declines, no fixed parameter set keeps it upright, so controller fragility rather than the adaptation method limits generalization to steeper terrain.
Coupling the agent to closed-loop attitude feedback and validating on hardware are natural next steps.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}

We showed that the CPG parameters producing stable quadruped locomotion shift with the terrain, so that a gait tuned for flat ground becomes unstable on an incline, and that across repeated transitions the terrain-matched optimum confers a durable survival advantage, so resuming stable walking calls for online adaptation.
We proposed an event-triggered scheme in which a prediction-error monitor detects each terrain change and an adaptation method re-tunes the CPG within the same walking bout, and we compared grid search, Bayesian optimization, and an autoregressive active inference agent as that method.
Online black-box search roughly doubles the fall rate by destabilizing the robot while probing candidate gaits, whereas the active inference agent selects from a learned model and avoids this harm; and once its goal prior encodes attitude stability rather than velocity, it significantly outperforms keeping the incumbent gait on a sustained transition, with the information-seeking term of the free energy essential to the gain.
Selecting parameters from a model rather than by physical trial avoids destabilization, and encoding stability in the agent's goal turns caution into improvement; making that improvement robust across recurring transitions, where the terrain optimum remains out of the model's reach, is the main direction for future work.
